# Historical Content Authoring Guide

This guide translates the historical framing in [game-design.md](game-design.md) into practical rules for writing cards, events, policies, interactions, and age-specific content. Data fields and validation rules remain in [content-schema.md](content-schema.md).

It is an authoring guide, not a new game system. Historical coherence should use the existing age schedule, prerequisites, standing developments, tags, injections, and event outcomes. Do not add a historical-scope taxonomy or a separate city-facts layer unless the design changes explicitly require one.

## 1. Setting Contract

Civitas Chronica follows a **composite European provincial town**, not a specific historical location and not a universal model of Europe.

- The town begins deliberately underdetermined.
- Developments chosen by the player gradually establish its geography, economy, institutions, and regional role.
- Later content must respect what the run has already established.
- The town may become prosperous, specialized, or culturally important, but it remains provincial. It is never a national capital or one of Europe's principal metropolises.
- Every resulting city should be historically plausible; no single run is intended to represent every European town.

Early ambiguity is intentional. Later contradiction is not. Once a run has established a navigable river, textile economy, garrison, religious center, mining hinterland, or administrative role, later cards and events should develop, transform, or explicitly supersede that history.

## 2. General Historical Rules

### Write from the town outward

Larger transformations normally appear through their local consequences:

- new laws, taxes, levies, or administrative standards;
- changing prices, trade routes, and imported goods;
- military recruitment, billeting, requisition, or refugees;
- roads, canals, railways, utilities, and communications;
- circulating publications and political ideas;
- technologies invented elsewhere but adopted, resisted, or felt locally;
- regional institutions being created, reformed, relocated, or withdrawn.

The town may adopt, resist, profit from, or be bypassed by change, but it is rarely the origin of a continent-wide movement.

### Preserve institutional variety

Do not imply that Europe had one uniform municipal constitution or sequence of reforms. Unless the setting is deliberately localized later, prefer functional descriptions such as:

- "the crown" or "the central government";
- "the provincial administration";
- "a new municipal code";
- "war on the frontier";
- "revolution in the capital";
- "the old charter" or "customary privileges."

Historical variation should emerge through institutions, choices, and consequences rather than interchangeable national decoration.

### Treat change as mixed

Useful developments should be allowed to create liabilities, conflicts, or new expectations.

- A printing house expands knowledge and political debate.
- A cadastral survey improves revenue while exposing unequal taxation.
- A railway connects the town, bypasses it, or makes it dependent on a regional center.
- Sanitation improves Health while creating debt, demolition, and displacement.
- Industrial employment improves Appeal while worsening Health.
- Heritage redevelopment improves Appeal while increasing housing pressure or Legitimacy.

Avoid a simple sequence in which later institutions are always morally and mechanically superior. Modern capacity creates new expectations and new failure modes.

### Demand activation is not the birth of a need

People cared about food, safety, justice, health, and the desirability of their home in every era. A demand activates when the concern becomes a persistent, city-wide municipal responsibility.

Earlier forms of that concern should still appear in cards and events:

- political negotiation before Legitimacy activates;
- medicine, plague control, poor relief, water, and waste before Health activates;
- migration, reputation, employment, comfort, and belonging before Appeal activates.

## 3. Age Briefs

### Age 1 — Charter and Consolidation, 1500–1600

The town establishes and defends its place in the surrounding region.

Suitable content includes:

- market rights and renewal of privileges;
- guild recognition and artisanal regulation;
- granaries, market halls, mills, roads, bridges, and river landings;
- parish institutions, hospitals, charity, and poor relief;
- walls, watches, fire regulations, and local musters;
- harvest failure, grain prices, fire, disease, and scarcity;
- negotiation among council, lord, crown, church, guilds, property holders, and inhabitants.

The town may be oligarchic and unequal, but it is not politically inert simply because Legitimacy is not yet an active meter.

### Age 2 — Confession, State, and Exchange, 1600–1750

The town is drawn into wider networks of belief, commerce, administration, knowledge, and war.

Suitable content includes:

- confessional settlements and religious conflict;
- stronger taxation, census-taking, and state administration;
- military levies, billeting, fortification, and wartime trade disruption;
- print, postal connections, schools, and practical learning;
- surveyors, physicians, engineers, apothecaries, and merchants;
- plague, inoculation precursors, fire, and changing public-order institutions;
- wider commercial networks, canals, turnpikes, and imported goods.

New knowledge normally arrives through people, publications, institutions, and practical techniques. The town need not be a center of the Scientific Revolution.

### Age 3 — Agrarian and Commercial Transformation, 1750–1850

The town remains closely tied to its rural hinterland while agriculture, markets, administration, and political expectations change.

Suitable common paths include:

- agricultural improvement, drainage, reclamation, and land consolidation;
- commercial farming and changing grain markets;
- population pressure and disputes over common resources;
- cottage textiles, putting-out networks, and artisanal production;
- roads, canals, improved river navigation, and periodic markets;
- censuses, cadastral surveys, standardized taxation, and conscription;
- poor relief, workhouse-like institutions, food protests, and customary-right disputes;
- pamphlets, petitions, revolutionary news, and municipal reform.

Factory industrialization is an optional specialization, not the default. It should normally require support from earlier transport, resource, trade, or manufacturing choices. Possible paths include mechanized mills, manufactories, foundries, canal depots, and early factory quarters.

Early labor organization belongs here through journeymen's associations, friendly societies, mutual-aid funds, illegal combinations, artisan strikes, and radical clubs. Durable mass unions and labor politics become a major Age 4 path.

### Age 4 — Urbanization and Civic Provision, 1850–1950

More people live and work within the town, and everyday life depends increasingly on civic systems.

Suitable content includes:

- railways, stations, workshops, or the consequences of being bypassed;
- workshops, light industry, factories, dense housing, and offices;
- water, sewers, refuse collection, paving, gas, and electricity;
- hospitals, schools, police, fire services, welfare offices, and municipal administration;
- pollution, workplace injury, infectious disease, overcrowding, and unsafe housing;
- newspapers, telegraph, telephone, organized labor, and mass elections;
- migration, outmigration, war, refugees, rationing, and requisition.

Heavy industry remains a specialization. Every town is nevertheless integrated into industrial society through manufactured goods, transport, migration, public standards, and expanding municipal responsibilities.

### Age 5 — Mobility, Services, and Competition, 1950–2030 (present day)

Greater mobility and communication make comparisons between places continuous.

Suitable content includes:

- motorization, bypasses, parking, public transport, and commuting;
- suburban housing, estates, redevelopment, and housing shortages;
- deindustrialization, factory closure, brownfields, and service employment;
- hospitals, colleges, regional administration, and consolidation of services;
- heritage, tourism, festivals, cultural quarters, and environmental quality;
- migration, integration, aging, youth outmigration, and population decline;
- shopping centers, empty high streets, business parks, and remote work;
- broadband, regional rail, and competition with larger urban centers.

A successful town might be a compact heritage center, a service hub, a comfortable commuter town, or an employment center. Size alone does not satisfy Appeal.

## 4. Demand-Specific Guidance

### Legitimacy

Legitimacy measures the gap between the authority claimed by the mayor and council and the authority inhabitants believe government deserves. It covers trust in government, acceptance of rules and burdens, demands for representation and liberty, and expectations of fairer treatment.

A low value means the current political settlement is broadly accepted. It does not imply democracy or equality: an oligarchic council, guild constitution, royal charter, or paternal administration may be accepted within the expectations of its period.

A high value means expectations have outgrown the existing settlement. Residents question who rules, whose interests government serves, how burdens are distributed, whose voice counts, and whether they could govern themselves better.

Demands for liberty, representation, or equal treatment are not themselves social damage. Legitimacy rises when institutions fail to answer those expectations.

Example relationships:

- **Printing House:** increases immediate Legitimacy pressure but opens newspaper and civic-debate paths.
- **Cadastral Survey:** improves Budget but raises Legitimacy if taxation remains unequal.
- **Conscription Office:** improves Security while worsening Legitimacy.
- **Published Municipal Accounts:** reduces Legitimacy pressure.
- **Elected Council:** reduces Legitimacy pressure but makes policy changes slower or more expensive.
- **Censorship Office:** may reduce the current meter while worsening its permanent growth step.
- **Factory Inspection:** improves both Health and Legitimacy at a Budget cost.
- **Luxury Redevelopment:** improves Appeal while worsening Legitimacy through displacement.

Knowledge and organization can destabilize an old settlement before enabling a more legitimate one.

### Health

Health covers the unmet requirements of healthy urban life:

- clean water and sanitation;
- waste removal and pollution control;
- safe housing and disease control;
- workplace safety;
- medical access;
- the minimum welfare provision needed to prevent concentrated hardship from becoming a public crisis.

Welfare affects Health when deprivation produces bodily urban consequences. It affects Legitimacy when inhabitants contest who receives help, how burdens are shared, or the conditions attached to assistance.

Example relationships:

- **Municipal Hospital:** reduces Health pressure.
- **Sewer Network:** strongly reduces Health pressure at high cost.
- **Tenement Blocks:** accelerate growth while worsening Health.
- **Poorhouse:** slightly improves Health but may worsen Legitimacy.
- **Public Assistance Office:** improves both Health and Legitimacy at a recurring Budget cost.
- **Model Housing:** improves Health but may displace residents during construction.
- **Coal Gas Works:** improves Budget while worsening Health.
- **Clean Water Board:** improves Health but creates bills or debt risk.

### Appeal

Appeal measures how strongly people choose this town over alternatives: whether they want to arrive, live there, and remain there, and whether they expect a comfortable and viable future.

It includes:

- housing and employment;
- services, mobility, and access;
- environmental quality and comfort;
- culture and social belonging;
- confidence in the town's future.

Appeal is comparative. Residents and prospective migrants judge the town against other towns, suburbs, regional centers, and major cities. A town can be safe and healthy yet still lose people because it feels stagnant, inconvenient, unaffordable, isolated, or without a future.

Appeal does not directly generate or spend population. Unmet Appeal contributes to the overall demand balance governing population growth.

Example relationships:

- **Suburban Housing Estate:** improves Appeal and Health while creating infrastructure or car-dependence costs.
- **Highway Bypass:** may improve Health or Security while worsening Appeal if commerce leaves the center.
- **Pedestrianized Old Town:** improves Appeal after a short-term Budget cost.
- **Regional University:** improves Appeal and Budget while creating housing pressure.
- **Industrial Park:** improves Appeal through employment while worsening Health.
- **Heritage Quarter:** improves Appeal while opening tourism and housing-cost events.
- **Commuter Rail:** improves Appeal but may turn the town into a dormitory settlement.
- **Affordable Housing Program:** improves Appeal at substantial Budget cost.
- **Factory Closure:** worsens Appeal and contributes to population decline.
- **Broadband Network:** improves Appeal and opens remote-work paths.

A high-Appeal town need not be large.

## 5. Historical Events

### Forced events are locally received pressures

Forced events may represent unavoidable historical change, but their title, text, choices, and effects should describe how that change reaches this town.

Use:

- "A new confessional settlement is imposed."
- "Pamphlets from the capital circulate in the market."
- "Mechanized cloth undercuts the guild workshops."
- "The army demands billets and recruits."
- "The railway company surveys a route through the district."
- "Refugees arrive from fighting elsewhere."
- "A national municipal code replaces the old charter."
- "The regional hospital is consolidated in a larger city."

Avoid:

- "The Reformation happens."
- "The Enlightenment arrives."
- "The Industrial Revolution begins."
- "The player causes the European Revolution."

A forced event must still offer locally meaningful choices. Wider history may be unavoidable, but the response and consequences for this town are not predetermined.

### Triggers and options

Event triggers prevent narrative impossibility rather than narrowly gating content. A flood needs a river; a dock strike needs docks; a plague needs no special prerequisite.

Standing developments should often change resolution rather than prevent an event from appearing:

- protection cancels an appropriate hazard;
- an institution unlocks a specific extra option;
- the same historical pressure produces different costs or opportunities in differently developed towns.

For example, a railway survey may be broadly scheduled, while existing Trade, Industrial, Infrastructure, or land-use choices determine which options appear and what they cost.

## 6. Authoring Checklist

Before accepting a card or event, verify:

- Does it describe a plausible pressure on a provincial town in this Age?
- Does it respect geography, institutions, industries, and earlier outcomes already established by the run?
- If it depicts a wider historical transformation, is the situation written as a local consequence?
- Does it avoid implying that all European towns followed one national or institutional sequence?
- Does it avoid treating a demand's activation as the first existence of that human concern?
- Are beneficial and harmful consequences mixed where historically and mechanically appropriate?
- Does Legitimacy measure institutional failure to meet expectations, rather than portraying demands for freedom as damage?
- Does welfare affect Health and Legitimacy through concrete consequences?
- Does Appeal include comfort, belonging, services, and comparative desirability rather than employment alone?
- If the content implies exceptional industrial, scientific, military, or cultural importance, have prior choices earned that path?
- Can the event's local choices be understood without the town becoming the center of continental history?

## 7. Pool and Balance Targets

Each Age currently targets approximately 60 card definitions. Only 20–45 belong in the base deck; the rest should be path injections, actions reached through events, or automatic supersession successors. A single run must not see every authored card.

Each Age must contain:

- at least 5 action cards;
- at least 10 ordinary events;
- at least 2 forced historical pressures;
- emergency and catastrophe events for every demand active by that Age;
- at least 6 developments that mitigate the newly activated demand;
- at least 3 developments that aggravate it.

Costs normally fall between 1 and 12. Cost 0 is reserved for automatic supersession successors, which are not drawn or played.

Balance is verified with `tools/verify.ps1`. Its deterministic matrix compares random, greedy, demand-aware, reckless, and passive play across the entire five-Age chain. The gate is intentionally broad: informed demand-aware play should usually win, but careless or nonresponsive play should not. Automated results are balance warnings, not substitutes for human playtests.
